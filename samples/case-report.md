# Severe Isolated Thrombocytopenia With Mucocutaneous Bleeding in an Adult: A Fictional Case Report

**Type** CARE-structured clinical case report  
**Intended audience** Hematologists, internal medicine clinicians, and trainees  
**Word count** Approximately 900, excluding references and portfolio note  
**Case status** Fully fictional. No patient records or identifiable information were used.

## Abstract

A 42-year-old woman presented with five days of spontaneous bruising, lower-limb petechiae, and intermittent gingival bleeding. Her platelet count was 8 × 10⁹/L, with preserved hemoglobin and leukocyte counts. Repeat sampling and peripheral blood film review confirmed isolated thrombocytopenia. The initial evaluation identified no secondary cause, and primary immune thrombocytopenia was the working diagnosis. She was admitted for observation and received a four-day course of oral dexamethasone. Gingival bleeding resolved by day 2, and the platelet count reached 76 × 10⁹/L by day 7. At six months, she remained free of bleeding and had required no additional treatment. The case illustrates how the blood film, bleeding phenotype, and follow-up inform diagnostic confidence and management.

## Introduction

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is a clinical diagnosis supported by isolated thrombocytopenia and the exclusion of plausible alternative causes. Initial evaluation combines the bleeding and medication history, physical examination, complete blood count, and peripheral blood film [1]. This fictional case is set in 2025 to place the treatment decision in its guideline context. The report follows the CARE structure [2].

## Patient information

A 42-year-old woman sought care after developing bruises without remembered injury and pinpoint lesions around both ankles. Her gums had bled twice during toothbrushing. Each episode stopped within minutes. She reported no epistaxis, heavy menstrual bleeding, gastrointestinal bleeding, hematuria, or headache. A complete blood count performed eight months earlier had shown a platelet count of 236 × 10⁹/L.

Her history included diet-controlled type 2 diabetes. She reported no new prescription or nonprescription medicines, antibiotic exposure, supplements, or quinine-containing products. There was no family history of thrombocytopenia or abnormal bleeding. She had no fever, weight loss, night sweats, arthralgia, or recent systemic illness.

## Clinical findings

On arrival, blood pressure was 118/74 mmHg, heart rate 78 beats/min, and temperature 36.8°C. Examination showed bilateral dependent petechiae and two ecchymoses on the thighs. There was no active oral bleeding, lymphadenopathy, or palpable hepatosplenomegaly. The neurologic examination was normal.

The platelet count was 8 × 10⁹/L, hemoglobin 13.1 g/dL, and leukocyte count 6.4 × 10⁹/L. Creatinine, bilirubin, lactate dehydrogenase, and coagulation studies were within the laboratory reference intervals.

## Diagnostic assessment

A sodium citrate sample yielded a platelet count of 9 × 10⁹/L. The blood film showed reduced platelet numbers with occasional large platelets and no clumping, schistocytes, or blasts. These findings supported genuine thrombocytopenia and made laboratory artifact and microangiopathic hemolysis less likely. The preserved hemoglobin and leukocyte counts, unremarkable film apart from the platelet findings, and absence of organomegaly supported deferring bone marrow examination [1].

HIV and hepatitis C screening were negative, and the pregnancy test was negative. The medication history and examination did not suggest a drug trigger, chronic liver disease, or systemic autoimmune disorder. Primary ITP remained the leading diagnosis. Persistent or recurrent thrombocytopenia accompanied by new cytopenias, abnormal cells, or systemic symptoms would have prompted reassessment.

## Intervention

The hematology team admitted the patient for observation because this was a new presentation with a platelet count below 20 × 10⁹/L and recent mucosal bleeding. She received oral dexamethasone 40 mg daily for four days without a taper. The team monitored capillary glucose because of her diabetes. Given the brief, self-limited bleeding, stable hemoglobin, and absence of a critical-site bleed, the team deferred intravenous immunoglobulin and platelet transfusion [1,3].

Before discharge, the team reviewed symptoms that would warrant urgent reassessment and arranged a repeat blood count and hematology follow-up.

## Outcomes

The patient completed all four doses. She reported transient insomnia and had temporary elevations in capillary glucose, neither of which required continued treatment after discharge. At day 7 she reported no new bruising or gingival bleeding. The platelet count remained within the laboratory reference interval at week 4 and month 6, without further ITP-directed therapy.

## Discussion

The case illustrates the value of confirming severe thrombocytopenia before attributing it to ITP. Repeat sampling addressed the possibility of platelet clumping, while film review and hemolysis markers helped assess whether the presentation was compatible with a thrombotic microangiopathy. The subsequent platelet rise was compatible with ITP, but a corticosteroid response alone cannot establish the diagnosis [1].

The four-day corticosteroid course reflected guidance available in 2025 [3]. In 2026, the American Society of Hematology issued a focused update conditionally favoring initial corticosteroids combined with either rituximab or a thrombopoietic agent over corticosteroids alone in adults with primary ITP [4]. The favorable outcome in this fictional case cannot establish comparative efficacy or predict long-term remission beyond the documented follow-up.

## Patient perspective

In the fictional account, the patient described the unexpected admission as more distressing than the bruising. She found the written instructions helpful because they clarified when to seek urgent care and when to expect her next blood count. She identified disrupted sleep as the most burdensome part of the corticosteroid course.

## Conclusion

Severe isolated thrombocytopenia with mucocutaneous bleeding warranted prompt evaluation and observation in this new presentation. Repeat sampling, blood film review, and assessment for secondary causes supported the working diagnosis. Follow-up documented resolution of bleeding, platelet recovery, and treatment tolerability.
