Advances in Multiple Myeloma Treatment From Diagnosis to Early Relapse

Title Advances in Multiple Myeloma Treatment From Diagnosis to Early Relapse
Type Evidence-based narrative review
Intended audience Hematologists, oncologists, and oncology fellows
Word count Approximately 1,050, excluding references and portfolio note

Abstract

Randomized phase III trials have expanded the role of anti-CD38 antibodies in newly diagnosed multiple myeloma and moved B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)–directed therapies into earlier relapse. This focused narrative review examines frontline quadruplets in transplant-eligible and transplant-ineligible adults, followed by chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy and bispecific antibody therapy after one to three prior lines. Progression-free survival improved across the populations studied, and updated CARTITUDE-4 results showed an overall survival benefit with ciltacabtagene autoleucel. Applying these findings requires attention to transplant fitness, prior anti-CD38 exposure and refractoriness, infection risk, and the time needed to deliver cellular therapy [1–5].

Introduction

Treatment selection in multiple myeloma increasingly depends on what a patient received before relapse. Frontline regimens may expose patients to an anti-CD38 antibody, a proteasome inhibitor, and an immunomodulatory drug during the same line. At progression, the duration of response and the drugs to which the disease is refractory become central to judging whether a relapse trial applies. This review focuses on selected randomized trials that address two decisions: how to intensify initial treatment and how to interpret evidence for BCMA-directed therapy at early relapse [1–5].

Current evidence

Frontline treatment in transplant-eligible adults

PERSEUS evaluated daratumumab added to bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone (VRd) during induction and consolidation, and to lenalidomide during maintenance. Both groups underwent autologous stem-cell transplantation. At 48 months, estimated progression-free survival was 84.3% with the daratumumab-containing strategy and 67.7% with VRd followed by lenalidomide maintenance. The hazard ratio for progression or death was 0.42 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.30–0.59). Serious adverse events occurred in 57.0% and 49.3% of patients, respectively. Because daratumumab was incorporated across several phases, the observed benefit reflects the full treatment strategy. The trial cannot isolate the contribution of daratumumab during induction, consolidation, or maintenance [1].

Frontline treatment in transplant-ineligible adults

IMROZ enrolled transplant-ineligible adults aged 18–80 years. Patients received isatuximab plus VRd followed by isatuximab, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone, or VRd followed by lenalidomide and dexamethasone. At a median follow-up of 59.7 months, estimated 60-month progression-free survival was 63.2% with isatuximab and 45.2% with the control strategy (hazard ratio, 0.60; 98.5% CI, 0.41–0.88). The result supports a quadruplet for patients resembling those enrolled. Its applicability is less certain in patients over 80 and in those whose functional limitations or comorbidities make a multiagent regimen difficult to deliver. Transplant ineligibility alone provides limited information about an individual patient’s ability to tolerate treatment [2].

CAR T-cell therapy at early relapse

CARTITUDE-4 enrolled patients with lenalidomide-refractory myeloma after one to three prior lines. It compared a strategy centered on ciltacabtagene autoleucel (cilta-cel) with investigator-selected pomalidomide, bortezomib, and dexamethasone or daratumumab, pomalidomide, and dexamethasone. At the primary analysis, estimated 12-month progression-free survival was 75.9% with cilta-cel and 48.6% with standard care (hazard ratio, 0.26; 95% CI, 0.18–0.38). The updated analysis also found longer overall survival with cilta-cel (hazard ratio for death, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.39–0.79). The treatment pathway included leukapheresis, bridging therapy, and lymphodepletion before infusion. Referral timing therefore matters, particularly when disease control during manufacturing is uncertain [3,4].

A bispecific antibody combination at early relapse

MajesTEC-3 compared teclistamab plus daratumumab with daratumumab and dexamethasone paired with either pomalidomide or bortezomib after one to three prior lines. Estimated 36-month progression-free survival was 83.4% with teclistamab plus daratumumab and 29.7% with the control regimens (hazard ratio, 0.17; 95% CI, 0.12–0.23). Grade 3 or 4 infections occurred in 54.1% and 43.4% of treated patients, respectively. The trial excluded patients with anti-CD38–refractory disease or prior BCMA-directed therapy. That exclusion directly affects how clinicians interpret the result for a patient whose disease progresses during frontline daratumumab [5].

Discussion

The frontline trials show that adding an anti-CD38 antibody can prolong disease control in two distinct treatment settings. Each trial evaluated a regimen delivered over several phases, and the populations differed in transplant eligibility and treatment exposure. Their progression-free survival estimates therefore describe separate clinical decisions. For a transplant-ineligible patient with baseline neuropathy, limited mobility, or recurrent infections, the expected benefit has to be weighed against the practical burden of delivering and monitoring a multiagent regimen over time [1,2].

At relapse, documenting whether progression occurred on treatment or after a treatment-free interval helps distinguish prior exposure from refractoriness. That distinction is particularly important for MajesTEC-3. Likewise, the numerical size of the progression-free survival benefit in MajesTEC-3 and CARTITUDE-4 cannot establish which BCMA-directed strategy is preferable. The studies used different eligibility criteria, control regimens, and assessment time points. They also leave open how best to sequence a bispecific antibody and CAR T-cell therapy in patients previously treated with a BCMA-directed agent [3–5].

The treatments pose different logistical and safety demands. CAR T-cell therapy requires coordination with a cellular therapy center and a plan for disease control before infusion. Teclistamab is available without autologous manufacturing, but step-up dosing and the infection burden make monitoring capacity and supportive care central to treatment planning. These practical differences can determine whether a trial-supported strategy is feasible for an individual patient [3–5].

Conclusion

Anti-CD38 quadruplets improved progression-free survival in the frontline populations studied, and BCMA-directed therapy improved disease control in selected patients at early relapse. The most useful starting points for applying these data are treatment fitness at diagnosis and a precise record of drug exposure and refractoriness at progression. The optimal sequence of BCMA-directed therapies remains unresolved [1–5].

A Randomized Phase II Trial of Dexamethasone De-escalation During Elotuzumab–Pomalidomide Therapy in Relapsed or Refractory Multiple Myeloma

Type Clinical trial protocol synopsis
Intended audience Clinical investigators, trial operations teams, and ethics reviewers
Word count Approximately 1,000, excluding references and portfolio note
Document status Fictional trial for portfolio purposes only. No study was conducted and no participants were enrolled.

Rationale

Elotuzumab plus pomalidomide and dexamethasone (EPd) improved progression-free survival compared with pomalidomide and dexamethasone in ELOQUENT-3 [1]. Repeated dexamethasone exposure can be difficult for patients who experience hyperglycemia, insomnia, or mood symptoms. This trial evaluates whether omitting dexamethasone on non-infusion weeks after two cycles of EPd reduces steroid exposure while preserving enough short-term disease control to justify a definitive trial. Both groups retain dexamethasone premedication on elotuzumab infusion days.

Objectives and endpoints

Primary objective. Estimate the between-group difference in disease control at 24 weeks after randomization. The primary endpoint is the proportion of participants alive at week 24 without International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG)–defined progression and without initiation of a new antimyeloma regimen. Death, documented progression, or initiation of a new regimen on or before week 24 constitutes failure [2].

Secondary objectives. Characterize progression-free survival, cumulative dexamethasone exposure, safety, and patient-reported symptoms. Secondary endpoints include time from randomization to progression or death, cumulative dexamethasone dose through week 24, grade 3 or higher adverse events, serious infections, infusion reactions, and changes in patient-reported sleep disturbance from randomization to weeks 12 and 24.

Design

Multicenter, open-label, randomized Phase II screening trial with a two-cycle EPd run-in. At the end of the run-in, participants with stable disease or better who can continue elotuzumab and pomalidomide will be randomized 1:1 through a central system. Randomization will be stratified by age (≤75 versus >75 years) and run-in response (stable disease versus partial response or better). An independent reviewer blinded to treatment assignment will apply IMWG criteria to progression assessments. Run-in attrition and its reasons will be reported separately [2].

Eligibility

Adults with measurable relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma may enter the run-in if they have received at least two prior lines, including lenalidomide and a proteasome inhibitor, and their treating clinician considers EPd an appropriate next regimen. Additional requirements include an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0–2, an absolute neutrophil count of at least 1.0 × 10⁹/L, a platelet count of at least 75 × 10⁹/L, and creatinine clearance of at least 30 mL/min.

Key exclusions include prior elotuzumab, pomalidomide-refractory disease, active uncontrolled infection, uncontrolled diabetes, a contraindication to the study drugs, and pregnancy or breastfeeding. Participants must comply with applicable pomalidomide pregnancy-prevention requirements. Patients who progress during the run-in leave the treatment phase and may start alternative therapy at their clinician’s discretion.

Intervention and assessments

During the run-in, both groups receive elotuzumab 10 mg/kg intravenously on days 1, 8, 15, and 22 of each 28-day cycle, pomalidomide 4 mg orally on days 1–21, and age-adjusted dexamethasone with infusion premedication. From cycle 3 onward, both groups receive elotuzumab 20 mg/kg intravenously on day 1 and continue pomalidomide on days 1–21 [3].

  • Control group. Continue age-adjusted dexamethasone on infusion day and oral dexamethasone on days 8, 15, and 22. The oral dose on non-infusion days is 40 mg for participants aged 75 years or younger and 20 mg for those older than 75 [3].
  • De-escalation group. Continue the same age-adjusted dexamethasone and other premedications on infusion day. Omit scheduled dexamethasone on days 8, 15, and 22.

Treatment continues until progression, unacceptable toxicity, withdrawal, or investigator decision. The protocol permits clinically indicated dose holds, reductions, and rescue corticosteroids. Sites will record their dates, doses, and reasons. Disease assessments occur at baseline, at randomization, and every four weeks through week 24, then every eight weeks through month 12 while the participant remains progression-free and has agreed to follow-up.

Safety monitoring and trial conduct

Sites will monitor blood counts, renal function, glucose, infections, thromboembolic events, and infusion reactions. The protocol will specify criteria for treatment interruption and resumption, risk-based thromboprophylaxis, and management of infusion reactions. Investigators will report serious adverse events to the sponsor within 24 hours of awareness, as specified in the protocol. The sponsor will assess expedited reporting obligations. An independent safety committee will review deaths, serious infections, severe infusion reactions, and early progression after the first 20 randomized participants complete eight weeks, with authority to recommend a pause or amendment [3,4].

Written informed consent is required before trial-specific procedures, following ethics committee approval and applicable regulatory authorization. Consent materials will explain the experimental schedule, alternatives, foreseeable risks, and the right to stop treatment or decline further follow-up. The sponsor will prioritize consent documentation, eligibility, treatment assignment, dosing, progression assessments, and serious adverse-event reporting in its risk-based monitoring plan. Study records will use coded identifiers, role-based access, and auditable corrections [4].

Statistical analysis

The trial will randomize 80 participants, 40 per group. The sample size supports estimation in a screening trial. With a 70% week-24 disease-control rate in each group, the approximate 95% confidence interval for the between-group difference would have a half-width of 20 percentage points. A formal noninferiority conclusion would require a larger trial and a prespecified, clinically justified margin.

The primary analysis will include all randomized participants in their assigned groups and report the absolute risk difference with a two-sided 95% confidence interval. Participants who discontinue assigned therapy will remain in follow-up where consent permits. Participants whose week-24 outcome cannot be established will count as failures in the primary analysis. A sensitivity analysis will vary the assumed outcomes for missing assessments by group. Safety summaries will include all participants who receive at least one dose after randomization, grouped by treatment received, with run-in safety reported separately. The decision to advance the strategy will consider the estimated loss of disease control and its uncertainty alongside steroid exposure, symptom burden, and serious adverse events.

Severe Isolated Thrombocytopenia With Mucocutaneous Bleeding in an Adult: A Fictional Case Report

Type CARE-structured clinical case report
Intended audience Hematologists, internal medicine clinicians, and trainees
Word count Approximately 900, excluding references and portfolio note
Case status Fully fictional. No patient records or identifiable information were used.

Abstract

A 42-year-old woman presented with five days of spontaneous bruising, lower-limb petechiae, and intermittent gingival bleeding. Her platelet count was 8 × 10⁹/L, with preserved hemoglobin and leukocyte counts. Repeat sampling and peripheral blood film review confirmed isolated thrombocytopenia. The initial evaluation identified no secondary cause, and primary immune thrombocytopenia was the working diagnosis. She was admitted for observation and received a four-day course of oral dexamethasone. Gingival bleeding resolved by day 2, and the platelet count reached 76 × 10⁹/L by day 7. At six months, she remained free of bleeding and had required no additional treatment. The case illustrates how the blood film, bleeding phenotype, and follow-up inform diagnostic confidence and management.

Introduction

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is a clinical diagnosis supported by isolated thrombocytopenia and the exclusion of plausible alternative causes. Initial evaluation combines the bleeding and medication history, physical examination, complete blood count, and peripheral blood film [1]. This fictional case is set in 2025 to place the treatment decision in its guideline context. The report follows the CARE structure [2].

Patient information

A 42-year-old woman sought care after developing bruises without remembered injury and pinpoint lesions around both ankles. Her gums had bled twice during toothbrushing. Each episode stopped within minutes. She reported no epistaxis, heavy menstrual bleeding, gastrointestinal bleeding, hematuria, or headache. A complete blood count performed eight months earlier had shown a platelet count of 236 × 10⁹/L.

Her history included diet-controlled type 2 diabetes. She reported no new prescription or nonprescription medicines, antibiotic exposure, supplements, or quinine-containing products. There was no family history of thrombocytopenia or abnormal bleeding. She had no fever, weight loss, night sweats, arthralgia, or recent systemic illness.

Clinical findings

On arrival, blood pressure was 118/74 mmHg, heart rate 78 beats/min, and temperature 36.8°C. Examination showed bilateral dependent petechiae and two ecchymoses on the thighs. There was no active oral bleeding, lymphadenopathy, or palpable hepatosplenomegaly. The neurologic examination was normal.

The platelet count was 8 × 10⁹/L, hemoglobin 13.1 g/dL, and leukocyte count 6.4 × 10⁹/L. Creatinine, bilirubin, lactate dehydrogenase, and coagulation studies were within the laboratory reference intervals.

Diagnostic assessment

A sodium citrate sample yielded a platelet count of 9 × 10⁹/L. The blood film showed reduced platelet numbers with occasional large platelets and no clumping, schistocytes, or blasts. These findings supported genuine thrombocytopenia and made laboratory artifact and microangiopathic hemolysis less likely. The preserved hemoglobin and leukocyte counts, unremarkable film apart from the platelet findings, and absence of organomegaly supported deferring bone marrow examination [1].

HIV and hepatitis C screening were negative, and the pregnancy test was negative. The medication history and examination did not suggest a drug trigger, chronic liver disease, or systemic autoimmune disorder. Primary ITP remained the leading diagnosis. Persistent or recurrent thrombocytopenia accompanied by new cytopenias, abnormal cells, or systemic symptoms would have prompted reassessment.

Intervention

The hematology team admitted the patient for observation because this was a new presentation with a platelet count below 20 × 10⁹/L and recent mucosal bleeding. She received oral dexamethasone 40 mg daily for four days without a taper. The team monitored capillary glucose because of her diabetes. Given the brief, self-limited bleeding, stable hemoglobin, and absence of a critical-site bleed, the team deferred intravenous immunoglobulin and platelet transfusion [1,3].

Before discharge, the team reviewed symptoms that would warrant urgent reassessment and arranged a repeat blood count and hematology follow-up.

Outcomes

The patient completed all four doses. She reported transient insomnia and had temporary elevations in capillary glucose, neither of which required continued treatment after discharge. At day 7 she reported no new bruising or gingival bleeding. The platelet count remained within the laboratory reference interval at week 4 and month 6, without further ITP-directed therapy.

Discussion

The case illustrates the value of confirming severe thrombocytopenia before attributing it to ITP. Repeat sampling addressed the possibility of platelet clumping, while film review and hemolysis markers helped assess whether the presentation was compatible with a thrombotic microangiopathy. The subsequent platelet rise was compatible with ITP, but a corticosteroid response alone cannot establish the diagnosis [1].

The four-day corticosteroid course reflected guidance available in 2025 [3]. In 2026, the American Society of Hematology issued a focused update conditionally favoring initial corticosteroids combined with either rituximab or a thrombopoietic agent over corticosteroids alone in adults with primary ITP [4]. The favorable outcome in this fictional case cannot establish comparative efficacy or predict long-term remission beyond the documented follow-up.

Patient perspective

In the fictional account, the patient described the unexpected admission as more distressing than the bruising. She found the written instructions helpful because they clarified when to seek urgent care and when to expect her next blood count. She identified disrupted sleep as the most burdensome part of the corticosteroid course.

Conclusion

Severe isolated thrombocytopenia with mucocutaneous bleeding warranted prompt evaluation and observation in this new presentation. Repeat sampling, blood film review, and assessment for secondary causes supported the working diagnosis. Follow-up documented resolution of bleeding, platelet recovery, and treatment tolerability.

Consent for publication. This case and the patient perspective are fictional. There was no patient from whom consent could be obtained.
